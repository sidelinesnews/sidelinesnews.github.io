What a CEO, a War and a Video Game Taught Me About Reading Power

A note from Sidelines · November 23, 2026

The bet

Last week the board of NVIDIA, one of the most valuable companies on earth, said it would not bring back its founder and CEO, Jensen Huang. The news said it. The experts said it. I made a friendly bet with an AI that he'd be back anyway.

Six days later, the board voted unanimously to reinstate him.

I'm not a stock genius. Most of my friends bet against me too, except one, who doesn't even follow markets. He just said: "Well, Steve Jobs came back to Apple."

That stuck with me. Why did a guy who doesn't play stocks see it, when the official line said the opposite?

What people say vs. what they need

The board said "never." But the company needed him more than it feared the scandal. There was no obvious successor, investors were nervous, and he had already offered to come back.

That last part was the tell. When someone powerful is already knocking on the door, and the people inside need him, "never" usually means "not yet."

So we wrote ourselves a few rules for reading the news:

  1. State the official line. What are they saying publicly?
  2. Ask what they need, not what they say. Public statements can be a bluff.
  3. Follow the power. Who can actually force the outcome?
  4. List what would have to be true for the unlikely thing to happen. Then ask how close we are.
  5. Look for a precedent. Has this happened before? (Thanks, Steve Jobs.)
  6. Look for the tell. One small fact usually gives it away.

Trying it on something harder

Then we tried the same rules on the war in Ukraine. Not to predict it. Nobody should pretend to. Just to see the shape of it.

Instead of asking what each leader says, ask what each one needs. One side needs to be able to call it a win at home. The other needs to know it won't be invaded again in five years. Europe needs a seat at the table. And the big powers want a deal they can take credit for.

When you look at it that way, peace stops being about who's right. It becomes about building an exit door that every side can walk through and still face their own people.

That doesn't make it easy. But it makes it visible.

The video game part

This weekend I played GTA 6 at a friend's place. To escape the police, changing your car isn't enough. You have to change your clothes, your route, everything that leaves a trail.

That's privacy online. Your phone location, the same username everywhere, your face in photos, your logins: every one is a link in the chain. Break enough links and they lose you.

Here's the line I can't shake:

Your wanted level online is always five stars. You just can't see it.

The point

Whether it's a CEO, a war, or your own data, the lesson is the same. Don't just listen to what's said. Look at what's needed, who holds the power, and what trail is being left.

That's what Sidelines is for: stepping outside the bubble long enough to see the shape of things.
